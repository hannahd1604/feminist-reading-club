Most of us either work in academia or studied at a university, which made this the session where the figures and the personal stories were hardest to keep apart. We started with what sexism actually is, using a continuum that runs from the everyday subtle kind that makes you wonder whether you are imagining it all the way through to harassment. Then we went to the numbers: KU Leuven's own scissor graph, the credit and citation gaps, the motherhood penalty, and what happens to women of colour in a system that already loses women early. The easy explanation for why women leave academia is children, so we spent most of the session on the harder ones — departmental culture, double standards, the service burden, and the absence of anyone senior who looks like you. We ended somewhere smaller and more practical than the problem: read each other's applications, practise each other's interviews, pass on the jobs and conferences we hear about.

<div class="section-head" id="structure">Session structure</div>
<ol class="structure-list">
  <li>What counts as sexism
    <ul>
      <li>Lisa Ann Richey's continuum model: from everyday subtle sexism that makes you think "is it just me, am I crazy?" through to sexual harassment</li>
      <li>Recognising it is the precondition for acting against it</li>
      <li>"Did this really happen to me" — the one that stuck with you</li>
      <li>#MeTooSTEM, and the #MeToo wave in Danish higher education</li>
    </ul>
  </li>
  <li>Racism in academia
    <ul>
      <li>The "inclusion tax": the various labours expected of you to be included in white spaces, and to resist or adhere to white social norms</li>
      <li>The minority tax</li>
      <li>The recent Jason Arday case</li>
    </ul>
  </li>
  <li>Going over the numbers
    <ul>
      <li>Gender Action Plans as the standard policy instrument since the 2010s</li>
      <li>KU Leuven: almost 50% of staff are women, but only 12% of professors in 2014 and 20% in 2020</li>
      <li>The university's position is that the scissor gap closes slightly each year and that this takes time because it only happens through promotion, which struck some of us as a lazy excuse even if the action plan helps</li>
      <li>Fewer women work full time</li>
      <li>The scissor graph looks completely different per faculty: extreme in biomedical sciences, essentially absent in science and engineering</li>
      <li>On women of colour in US academia there is very little research at all, and what exists shows underrepresentation starting early and accumulating drastically</li>
      <li>What that research does investigate is the sense of belonging: lack of role models, solo status, and difficulty forming relationships with senior colleagues</li>
      <li>Across every group, women report having to work harder, having their work valued less by their department, and significantly more subtle discrimination</li>
      <li>Women also carry more of the workload with students, meetings, and non-research work</li>
      <li>Professional isolation, mediated by department climate, comes out as a driver of women of colour leaving STEM</li>
      <li>The myth of historical bias versus the leaky pipeline, and which of the two the papers actually support</li>
    </ul>
  </li>
  <li>Output and credit
    <ul>
      <li>Women in research teams are significantly less likely than men to be credited with authorship, with real consequences for their careers</li>
      <li>This is not a productivity gap: women are not less productive, their work is undervalued</li>
      <li>Men cite their own papers 56% more than women do, and citations compound into impact and further citations</li>
      <li>Papers by women stay longer under review: 9% longer for mixed teams, 20% for all-female ones</li>
    </ul>
  </li>
  <li>Motherhood in academia
    <ul>
      <li>From a Nature paper on Danish academia: women take on five times more childcare responsibility, and spend more time on every individual childcare task</li>
      <li>Motherhood penalties in pay and in promotion, still present after the first academic job post-PhD</li>
      <li>The effect exists in other sectors too, but bites harder in academia because the career is almost entirely output-focused</li>
      <li>The penalty is more pronounced in departments with no women in senior positions</li>
    </ul>
  </li>
  <li>Why do women leave academia?
    <ul>
      <li>The easy answer is children, but the real answer is institutional barriers and culture</li>
      <li>Double standards: higher expectations of women in community service and in article quality, plus the service burden</li>
      <li>Internalised misogyny — why do women in academia turn on other women, why are prolific female scientists so often perceived as evil, and why are some of them actually evil?</li>
      <li>Departmental culture, confidence and imposter syndrome, and the absence of role models</li>
      <li>We shared personal experiences here, including why each of us chose, or did not choose, academia as a workplace</li>
    </ul>
  </li>
  <li>Do DEI efforts work?
    <ul>
      <li>The increase in Asian staff in the US is driven mainly by hiring foreign-born people</li>
      <li>There was a brief effect of DEI in post-pandemic hiring, and it did not last</li>
      <li>The claims of a "lost generation" of white male hires, or of a massive boost to minority applicants, are simply unsupported by the data</li>
    </ul>
  </li>
  <li>So what do we do?
    <ul>
      <li>Report? Only 7% of students and 23% of staff report gender-based violence incidents at their institution</li>
      <li>The European Commission's 2024 zero-tolerance code of conduct</li>
      <li>Mentoring?</li>
      <li>Support each other concretely, with jobs in mind: read each other's applications, practise interviews, share job and conference calls — possibly as a group of its own</li>
    </ul>
  </li>
</ol>
